# Camera-based patient positioning and positioning-error detection for conventional projection radiography

Literature review for a webcam-based (RGB) positioning copilot project. Compiled August 2026 from PubMed, arXiv/OpenReview, publisher sites, and vendor documentation. Each claim carries an inline citation with title, venue/year, and URL.

## Summary

The field splits into two largely disconnected literatures. On the acquisition side, vendors ship camera-assisted positioning today: Agfa's SmartXR projects the collimation field and AEC cells onto the patient in augmented reality, gives tube-to-panel alignment feedback, and suggests exposure technique from depth-derived body thickness, while Siemens-style time-of-flight cameras on CT tables have strong peer-reviewed evidence of cutting centering error roughly in half versus radiographers. Academic prototypes for plain radiography mostly attach depth or ToF cameras to the X-ray tube and train networks to predict, before exposure, whether the resulting radiograph would be diagnostic; this works in early studies but rests almost entirely on synthetic training data. On the image side, there is mature work detecting positioning errors after the fact from the radiograph itself: rotation from clavicle/spinous-process geometry, inspiration level, collimation, and scapular overlap all classify with AUCs around 0.9 to 0.99, and one before/after study reports a 30% relative increase in optimal-quality chest images when AI feedback is installed at the acquisition console. Repeat/reject analysis consistently shows positioning is now the dominant reject cause in digital radiography (about 49 to 77 percent of rejects), which is exactly the failure mode a pre-exposure camera assistant targets. What nobody has published is a validated plain-RGB-camera system giving real-time centering, rotation, and symmetry feedback before exposure, with prospective evidence that it reduces rejects.

## 1. Agfa SmartXR

SmartXR is Agfa's portfolio of acquisition-time assistance tools, available on the DR 600 fixed room and DR 100s mobile unit ("SmartXR Intelligent Workflow", Agfa Radiology Solutions product page, 2023, https://agfaradiologysolutions.com/products/smartxr-intelligent-workflow/). The components relevant to positioning:

- SmartPositioning overlays the live collimation area and the positions of the automatic exposure control (AEC) cells onto a live camera view of the patient, displayed on the Tube Head Display, so the radiographer can verify coverage remotely before exposure. It also flags active cells blocked by shields or not covered by the patient ("SmartXR - Visual User Assistance for Digital Radiography", Agfa, 2023, https://agfaradiologysolutions.com/en-in/benefits/visual-user-assistance-ind/).
- SmartAlign gives real-time feedback on tube-to-panel alignment during bedside and out-of-bucky exams to avoid grid cut-off and angle inconsistency.
- SmartDose uses what Agfa calls "3D machine vision" to measure patient thickness and propose tailored exposure parameters before exposure.
- SmartRotate uses a deep neural net to auto-orient images post-acquisition.
- LiveVision is the first-person camera stream feeding SmartPositioning and SmartDose.

Sensors required: integrated cameras on the tube head plus additional alignment sensors; Agfa describes the platform as "a combination of integrated sensors and software... 3D machine vision, deep learning and machine intelligence" ("Business Case and Benefits of Automatic X-ray Rotation Functionality within Agfa's SmartXR Offering", Frost & Sullivan white paper hosted by Agfa, https://www.agfa.com/he/global/en/internet/he/library/libraryopen?ID=81678309). The company has also filed patent activity around camera-based chest orientation estimation using clavicle-to-spinous-process distance measured from a camera image rather than the radiograph ("Apparatus for determining an orientation of a patient's chest", US Patent 12400357, https://exa.ai/library/legal/patent/0qmq807pncbdh0080fd16h).

Validation status: thin, and mostly vendor-produced. The quantitative claims circulating (manual image rotations reduced from 46% to 4-5% of exposures at Rush Medical Imaging Center, 2020-2021; manual rotations down from 17.6% to 3.4% across ten AdventHealth systems, 2021-2022) come from the Agfa-commissioned Frost & Sullivan white paper, i.e., marketing-grade case studies, not peer-reviewed trials (same URL as above). The HealthManagement.org interview describing the AR AEC overlay is explicitly part of Agfa's "Corporate Engagement" program ("Point of Care AI for Digital Radiography", HealthManagement.org, 2021, https://healthmanagement.org/c/healthmanagement/IssueArticle/point-of-care-ai-for-digital-radiography).

The only independent evaluation I found is a 2025 Universidad El Bosque dissertation measuring SmartRotate, SmartPositioning, and SmartDose on a DR 600 at the Santa Fe de Bogotá Foundation (Forero Lozano, "Measuring the impact of the 'SmartXR' artificial intelligence software...", Universidad El Bosque repository, 2025, https://repositorio.unbosque.edu.co/items/67d5d9c6-bcb2-45f5-97e8-86b4bf6b138d). In this single-center before/after cohort: rotated-image rate fell from 96% to 0% for the SmartRotate comparison (161 studies, p < 0.001); positioning-related rejects dropped by roughly 36 to 41 percent depending on exam group (wall-bucky spine/shoulder 41%, lower limbs 37%, chest 36%) between January-May 2023 and January-May 2024; SmartDose was associated with 1,075 avoided exposures over five months. This is a student dissertation, not a peer-reviewed journal article, so treat the numbers as indicative only. No randomized or peer-reviewed journal validation of SmartPositioning's AR overlays exists as of this writing.

## 2. Depth/time-of-flight camera prototypes for patient centering

CT is where the evidence is strongest. The commercial system evaluated in these papers is the infrared ToF camera + RGB camera mounted at the gantry, with a statistical shape model ("avatar") fitted to the point cloud to estimate the patient's isocenter; it underpins Siemens' FAST SupC / FAST 3D Positioning workflow family (the PIAA paper below confirms FAST stands for "Fully Assisting Scanner Technologies").

Key results:

- Booij et al., "Accuracy of automated patient positioning in CT using a 3D camera for body contour detection", European Radiology 2019 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6420476/). 423 patients positioned manually vs 254 by the camera. Median absolute table-height deviation from ideal isocenter: 13.2 mm for radiographers vs 6.1 mm for the camera (p < 0.0005); per-region medians 5.4-9.5 mm for the camera. Describes the full algorithm: ToF depth image, point cloud, pose/body-part detection, fallback isocenter between point cloud and tabletop, then avatar fit robust to blankets and positioning aids.
- Saltybaeva et al., "Precise and automatic patient positioning in computed tomography: trajectory/offset study", European Journal of Radiology 2018 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29762259/). 120 patients: mean vertical offset 19 ± 10 mm manual vs 5 ± 3 mm automatic; chest 19 → 7 mm, abdomen 18 → 4 mm; worst-case offset never exceeded 15 mm with the camera vs 39-43 mm manually. Larger patients (> BMI 26) were disproportionately mispositioned manually.
- Booij et al., pediatric extension, European Radiology 2020 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32749591/). 191 children: e.g., abdomen median deviation 10.2 mm (radiographer) vs 3.1 mm (camera), thorax 9.7 vs 4.8 mm.
- "Use of a 3D camera for automated patient positioning for chest-abdomen-pelvis CT scans: effect on positioning accuracy and patient dose", J Appl Clin Med Phys 2024 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1078817424000932). Median vertical offset improved from 17 to 8 mm (thorax, p = 0.001) and 16 to 7 mm (abdomen-pelvis, p = 0.003); share of patients within 5 mm of isocenter rose from 16.1% to 39.0%. Notably, no significant dose difference as a function of water-equivalent diameter was measured at their site, so the dose benefit remains inferred rather than demonstrated.

GE, Canon, Philips, and United Imaging sell comparable camera/AI positioning assistance for CT (United Imaging markets contactless positioning), but published validation for those specific products is sparse; the PIAA authors survey this landscape ("PIAA: Pre-imaging all-round assistant for digital radiography", Technology and Health Care, 2024, https://doi.org/10.3233/thc-240639).

For plain radiography specifically:

- University of Lübeck group (Laufer, Mairhöfer, Barth, Martinetz, Barkhausen et al.). They mount two ToF cameras on the X-ray device and train networks to predict, from the depth image alone, whether the upcoming radiograph will be diagnostic. Foundation paper: "Patient Pose Assessment in Radiography using Time-of-Flight Cameras", SPIE Medical Imaging 2024, vol. 12926 (cited in the OpenReview PDF below). Follow-ups: camera-placement study over 461,550 synthetic depth images and 50 candidate positions, finding the position directly perpendicular above the target anatomy is best (87.84% diagnostic accuracy for upper-ankle poses) and a second camera does not help ("Evaluation of Time-of-Flight Camera Positioning for AI-based Patient Pose Assessment in Radiography", OpenReview/NeurIPS submission, 2025, https://openreview.net/forum?id=w7D40Vc1b6); an ESANN 2024 paper applies the same cameras to collimation optimization (referenced in the same PDF); and a synthetic-data framework generating paired depth/radiograph images from CT scans, released publicly, improves real-data pose assessment by up to 11 points to 90.45%, close to the ~93% achievable when judging from the actual radiographs ("Patient Pose Assessment Using a CT-Based Framework for Synthetic Data Generation", arXiv, 2026, https://arxiv.org/html/2608.06126v1).
- Washington University prototype predates these: a Microsoft Kinect v2 mounted on the tube housing of a Philips DigitalDiagnost room projected ordered body part, receptor position, thickness, AEC cell locations, collimated field, motion, and suggested technique onto an AR view. Volunteer-only feasibility, no exposures acquired ("Improving X-Ray Imaging with Computer Vision and Augmented Reality", AAPM 2016 abstract SU-C-209-06, MacDougall et al., https://www.aapm.org/meetings/2016am/PRAbs.asp?aid=32681&mid=115).
- PIAA (Southeast University + Careray): RGB-D multi-camera array plus embedded compute that detects body part, estimates 2.5D pose, and proposes exposure area/kVp/mAs for five DR views. On 30 volunteers, suggested parameters were accepted in 85 of 86 trials, and positioning-plus-exposure-prep time fell from 73 s to 8 s (Technology and Health Care, 2024, URL above). Volunteer study, no clinical outcome measures yet.

## 3. Detecting positioning errors from the radiograph itself

### Rotation

The textbook criterion is that the medial clavicular heads should be equidistant from the thoracic spinous processes on a PA/AP chest radiograph (references surveyed in Santosh et al. below). Two caveats matter for anyone automating this: Hardy, Scotland and Herron showed body morphology changes how rotation appears on PA radiographs, so a population-level assumed clavicle-spinous distance does not generalize per patient ("Assessing Sagittal Rotation on Posteroanterior Chest Radiographs: The Effect of Body Morphography on Radiographic Appearances", Journal of Medical Imaging and Radiation Sciences 46, 2015; discussed in the US patent above), and clavicle-head boundary detection itself is fragile (Santosh et al.).

- Santosh, Candemir, Jaeger, Karargyris, Antani, Thoma, Folio, "Automatically Detecting Rotation in Chest Radiographs Using Principal Rib-Orientation Measure for Quality Control", Int J Pattern Recognition and Artificial Intelligence 29(2), 2015 (work from 2014; https://lhncbc.nlm.nih.gov/LHC-publications/PDF/pub8970.pdf). Avoids clavicle detection entirely by comparing principal rib orientations between lungs; about 90% accuracy on the Indiana and Montgomery County datasets. Early, pre-deep-learning, and explicitly aimed at QC during acquisition.
- von Berg et al., "Robust chest x-ray quality assessment using convolutional neural networks and atlas regularization", SPIE Medical Imaging 11313, 2020 (abstract: https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020SPIE11313E..1LV/abstract; expanded ECR 2020 poster C-05625: https://epos.myesr.org/poster/esr/ecr2020/C-05625). Siemens work: CNNs plus probabilistic anatomical atlases localize lung borders, posterior ribs, diaphragm, clavicle heads, spinous processes, then compute collimation, rotation-asymmetry, and inhalation figures. Landmark errors on 142 unseen cases: clavicle-head position ~2.5-4.7 mm; inhalation rib-count index error 0.01 ± 0.27 ribs. Runs in under a second on CPU. This is essentially the measurement engine behind later Siemens feedback deployments.
- "Learning Patient Rotation Using Synthetic X-ray Images from 3D CT Volumes", OpenReview workshop paper (~2023; https://openreview.net/pdf?id=rE5kyC31IXQ). DenseNet-121 regresses internal rotation angle from synthetic CXRs forward-projected from photon-counting CT volumes at 2° steps; R² = 0.992 with 95% limits near ±2°. Trained/tested purely synthetically; real-image validation listed as future work. Useful precedent for the synthetic-training approach a public-data project would need.
- "What patient positioning in chest X-ray is still acceptable? An empirical study on thresholding quality metrics", Leiden/Utrecht (2024; https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/handle/1887/4178475). Applied AI estimates of collimation distance, clavicle balance, and ribs-above-diaphragm to 64,315 ChestX-ray8 images, sampled 920 for expert annotation, and derived thresholds reproducing expert okay/inadequate/unacceptable ratings. Separating acceptable from unacceptable: collimation AUC > 0.97, rotation AUC = 0.93, inspiration AUC = 0.97. Also documents that expert agreement on "inadequate" is only fair to moderate, which bounds any supervised system.
- Chinese RFFN study, "Application of multi-scale feature extraction and explainable machine learning in chest x-ray position evaluation within an integrated learning framework", European Radiology 2025 (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00330-025-12097-9). U-net++ segments lung fields, scapulae, clavicles (mean Dice 0.926); five quantitative indicators feed a random-forest fusion classifier, overall positioning-classification AUC 0.982 on 3300 local CXRs. SHAP analysis ranks scapula-lung overlap as the top indicator. Notably finds that a rigid 4.5° clavicle-angle threshold produces false alarms in the 0-6° range, echoing the morphology problem above.
- "Application of deep learning strategies in the standardization..." BMC Medical Imaging 2026 (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12880-026-02278-3). Faster R-CNN + ResNet-50 pipeline on 3663 PA CXRs checks three compliance criteria: cervical-spine visibility accuracy 94.54%, costophrenic-angle completeness 98.06%, scapular non-overlap ~92%.

### Inspiration

- Nousiainen, Mäkelä, Piilonen, Peltonen, "Automating chest radiograph imaging quality control", Physica Medica 83:138-145, 2021 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejmp.2021.03.014). Six CNNs on 2589 standing PA CXRs judge lung inclusion at four edges, rotation, and inspiration. AUCs > 0.88 (inclusion), > 0.70 (rotation), > 0.79 (inspiration). Human interobserver agreement for inspiration was itself only 89%, and network confidence tracked human agreement, a useful calibration finding.
- "Retaking assessment system based on the inspiratory state of chest X-ray image", Radiological Physics and Technology, 2025 (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12194-025-00888-0). Neat labeling trick: derives retake/not-retake labels from dynamic digital radiography series, trains VGG16 transfer-learned models, and reaches 82.3% accuracy on 48 static real CXRs (recall 0.873 for insufficient inspiration). Small numbers, but directly frames inspiration as a retake-decision problem.
- Melbourne group, "Assessment of inspiration and technical quality in anteroposterior thoracic radiographs using machine learning", 2024 (record: https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1925955). CNN trained on 2375 radiologist-labelled AP films (25.5% technically inadequate, 36.6% inadequate inspiration among adequate-technique images); addresses the AP/mobile setting most QC work ignores.

### Full pipelines and measured clinical impact

- von Berg et al. (Siemens), "Impact of AI-based Real Time Image Quality Feedback for Chest Radiographs in the Clinical Routine", medRxiv 2021 (https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.06.10.21258326v1). Deployed the SPIE-2020 measurements as feedback at the console; relative increases of 30% in optimal-quality images overall (collimation +28%, inspiration +4%), and 34% reduction in unnecessarily exposed area. Before/after design at presumably one site, but the closest thing to outcome evidence that post-hoc QC feedback changes practice.
- Usui, Yoshimura, Ichikawa, Sugimori, "Development of Chest X-ray Image Evaluation Software Using the Deep Learning Techniques", Applied Sciences 13, 2023 (https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4418/13/3/497 link is Diagnostics; the Appl Sci paper: https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/applsci/applsci-13-06695/article_deploy/applsci-13-06695.pdf). Retake-decision software on 4809 ChestX-ray8 images: classifiers for lung-field defects, obstructing objects, and object location score 89.8-91.7% accuracy; lung segmentation mIoU 0.920; total response 36 ms/image.

## 4. Estimating patient thickness / habitus from camera images for exposure selection

- Vendor side, Agfa SmartDose is the shipped example: depth-based thickness drives exposure-parameter suggestions, with vendor-cited motivation that incorrect exposure accounts for ~18% of retakes and a lean patient given standard technique can receive up to six times the needed dose (HealthManagement.org interview and SmartXR brochure, both URLs in section 1). No peer-reviewed accuracy study found; flag as marketing until section 1's independent dissertation-grade data matures.
- Strongest academic phantom result: RGB-D thickness for entrance-skin-dose estimation, PubMed 41033267, 2025 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41033267/). Intel RealSense D435i mounted on a radiography unit imaging abdominal phantoms (20-40 cm): thickness error ≤ ±0.3 cm at detector center, ≤ ±1.2 cm off-center; ESD estimates from camera-derived thickness differed from caliper-derived truth by ≤ 0.4 mGy (±0.7%). Feasibility-grade but quantitatively convincing.
- Japanese system combining pose estimation and depth for technique selection: "Estimation of Shooting Part Using a Camera with Depth Sensors and Pose Estimation Method and Automatic Setting of Optimal X-ray Imaging Conditions", Japanese Journal of Radiological Technology 79(5), 2023 (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjrt/79/5/79_2023-1341/_article/-char/en). OpenPose-based body-part recognition worked far better from the RGB stream (100% at 120 cm) than from depth alone (15-42%); subject thickness measured within ±10 mm except outliers. Directly relevant to the RGB-vs-depth question.
- Earlier Kinect-era work established the concept: whole-body volume from Kinect depth correlates with weight with arm-position dependence ("Using the Microsoft Kinect for Patient Size Estimation and Radiation Dose Normalization", J Digit Imaging / PMC3705007, 2013, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3705007/); a Kinect 1.0 wall-stand prototype reported 1 mm display precision for thickness at the central ray plus traffic-light AEC-coverage feedback (Don et al., RSNA 2015 abstract SSK16-02, https://www.rsna.org/uploadedFiles/RSNA/Content/Press/pr2015/don/abstract/Don.pdf); and the AAPM 2016 Kinect-v2 AR prototype above included thickness-based technique suggestion.
- Adjacent: RGB-D anatomy registration estimating chest thickness and lung position for tomosynthesis planning ("Anatomy Registration via Patient Sensing for Chest X-ray Digital Tomosynthesis", SPIE 2023, https://yangece.github.io/papers/conference/SPIE2023-Anatomy-Registration.pdf).
- Closest published ancestor of an RGB-webcam copilot: "Deep Learning-Based Estimation of Radiographic Position to Automatically Set Up the X-Ray Prime Factors", 2025 (record: https://repisalud.isciii.es/entities/publication/6a2ebbc0-ca4f-4ea7-8cae-caebc1b4fcf8/full; PMC12092909). A lightweight ConvNeXt classifies the intended radiographic view from a photograph taken before exposure (66 positions, 75 volunteers, two facilities) and maps it to kVp/mAs/SID suggestions: 93.17% view-classification accuracy, 95.58% correct prime-factor selection since many confusions are clinically harmless. Volunteers only; no thickness estimation; no patient outcomes. Habitus-from-photo for dose remains unvalidated territory.

## 5. Repeat/reject analysis in plain film radiography

The headline trend is well documented in AAPM Task Group 305 ("Guidance for standardization of vendor-neutral reject analysis in radiography", J Appl Clin Med Phys, 2023, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10161059/): screen-film reject rates ran around 11% with exposure errors dominating (40-45%); CR cut rates dramatically (one classic comparison: 27.6% film-screen vs 2.3% CR, "Comparative reject analysis in conventional film-screen and digital storage phosphor radiography", PubMed 10525892, 1999, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10525892/); but DR brought rates back up to roughly 8-15%, and the leading cause flipped from exposure to positioning.

Representative DR-era studies:

- Australia, emergency department: 90,298 images, reject rate 9%; causes: positioning 49%, anatomy cut-off 21% ("Reject rate analysis in digital radiography: an Australian emergency imaging department case study", J Med Radiat Sci 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31318181/).
- Norway, direct DR: 27,284 images, 12% rejected; knees, shoulders, wrist worst; 77% of rejects from positioning ("Reject analysis in direct digital radiography", PubMed 22287147, 2012, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22287147/).
- US server-based CR/DR audit: 66,063 images, monthly reject rate 8-10% (mean 8.7%); positioning 77.3% of rejects, exposure 9.8% ("One Year's Results from a Server-Based System for Performing Reject Analysis and Exposure Analysis in Computed Radiography", J Digit Imaging / PMC3056967, 2011, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3056967/).
- Egypt, DR: 2,192 images, repeat rate 8.5%; positioning 48.9%, centering 18.8%, exposure 8.6%; extremities repeated most, patients over 65 highest by age ("Analysis of image repetition rates and causes in digital radiology", Egyptian J Radiol Nucl Med, 2025, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s43055-025-01620-z).
- Saudi Arabia, DR: 89,797 images, 15% rejection; positioning dominant, artifact 28.5% and motion 17.1% of rejects; pelvis, abdomen, spine, knee above average ("Direct digital radiograph. Technicians role in obtaining good images", PubMed 25129192, 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25129192/).
- Namibia, CR: 8% reject rate; positioning 63%, exposure 24.9%; chest exams had the highest departmental reject share ("The causes of reject images in a radiology department at a state hospital in Windhoek, Namibia", South African Radiographer, https://sar.org.za/index.php/sar/article/view/424).
- Taiwan (as cited in the PIAA paper): 98,503 images, 4.89% retake rate; positioning/anatomical cutoff 56.1%, artifacts 20.6%, motion 12.0%, equipment 7.4%, exposure 4.0% (Lin et al., cited at https://doi.org/10.3233/thc-240639).

Consensus reading: modern digital departments run roughly 5-15% reject/repeat rates, positioning plus centering explains half to three quarters of them, and every author names positioning feedback and training as the remedy. That is the opportunity statement for a pre-exposure copilot.

## Gaps

1. No validated plain-RGB (webcam) positioning assistant. Every serious prototype uses depth: ToF cameras (Lübeck group), structured light/Kinect (Washington University), RGB-D arrays (PIAA, Japanese JJRT work). The single RGB-only study classifies view and kVp/mAs from a photo but does no geometric centering/rotation/symmetry feedback and never touched patients. Nothing published predicts radiographic rotation, centering offset, or symmetry from an ordinary webcam before exposure.
2. No peer-reviewed validation of commercial AR positioning suites. Agfa SmartXR's AR AEC-cell projection has zero peer-reviewed studies; evidence is one commissioned white paper and one university dissertation. Same pattern for other vendors' radiography-side features, in contrast to CT camera positioning, which has four solid multi-hundred-patient studies.
3. No closed-loop evidence connecting pre-exposure camera guidance to reject-rate or dose outcomes in plain radiography. The CT literature links camera centering to offset and (weakly) dose; the CXR QC literature shows post-exposure feedback improving image quality by ~30% relative. Nobody has published the combination for radiography: camera sees the pose, warns the radiographer, reject rate falls.
4. No public paired dataset of camera views with corresponding radiographs and positioning ground truth. The Lübeck group released synthetic ToF/radiograph pairs for ankle joints only (GitHub via arXiv 2026); chest, pelvis, and spine have nothing, and their own papers document why collecting real bad-pose data is ethically and legally hard. Synthetic-from-CT generation is the emerging workaround and is directly reusable.
5. Rotation metrics remain morphology-confounded. Since Hardy 2015, it is known the clavicle-spinous-distance heuristic breaks across body shapes, and the European Radiology 2025 paper shows rigid angular thresholds misfire below 6°. Few systems estimate absolute rotation angle robustly across habitus; the synthetic-projection approach gets ±2° but only in simulation.
6. Thickness-to-technique from cameras stops at phantoms and volunteers. Best result is ±0.3 cm on a flat phantom with RealSense; no study shows camera-derived technique charts reducing exposure-error retakes in a clinical series.
