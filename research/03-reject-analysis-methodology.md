# Reject and Repeat Rate Analysis in Digital Projection Radiography: Definitions, Methodology, Benchmarks, and AI State of the Art

**Purpose.** This review answers how departments and professional bodies define, classify, measure, and report reject and repeat rates in digital (DR) projection radiography, with the concrete numbers available for chest PA and pelvis AP exams, so that a real-time positioning copilot can be evaluated against metrics the field actually uses. All sources are primary (AAPM reports, IEC standards, peer-reviewed journals, one medRxiv preprint) unless noted. Publication years are given with every claim.

## Summary

The field's authoritative taxonomy is AAPM Task Group Report 305 (2023), which standardizes the reject definition, the denominator, and a coarse reject-reason schema so rates are comparable across vendors; crucially, it defines *rejects* (acquisitions never sent to a radiologist) and *repeats* (additional radiographs beyond the protocol's minimum views, all of which go to the radiologist) as distinct things, and puts repeats out of scope of the standard. In practice, most published DR "reject rates" are *deletion rates* (rejected images / total images on a workstation or in PACS), computed per image rather than per exposure actuation, with denominator conventions that vary between studies. Published overall DR reject rates cluster in the high single digits to low teens: 9.1% median across 44 Australian systems (Serra et al., 2024), 7.9% and 5.9% at two New Zealand departments (Bantas et al., 2023), 10.3% in an Australian emergency room (Oudiz et al., 2021), and 11% deletion rate in Norway (Hofmann et al., 2015), against a film-era baseline of roughly 10-15% and early CR rates near 5%. Across every DR study, *patient positioning* (often including centering and anatomy cut-off) is the dominant rejection cause, typically 50-80% of all rejects, replacing the exposure errors that dominated screen-film practice. Chest and pelvis are both high-reject projections, but their reported numbers diverge sharply by how they are measured: PA chest sits around 5% deletion (Oudiz), while AP chest (often portable/supine) reaches 18%; pelvis AP ranges from about 8% (CR, Foos 2009) up to 35.5% for trauma-trolley acquisitions (Lee et al., 2025). AI work on reject automation is nascent and almost entirely *post-exposure* image classification (chest, shoulder); no published work predicts reject-worthiness *before* exposure from a live camera, which is exactly the copilot's gap. Exposure index / deviation index (IEC 62494-1:2008; AAPM TG-116 2009; AAPM TG-232 2018) provide a standardized detector-exposure feedback loop used for QC and for validating reject reasons, but EI/DI are corrupted by positioning and are not a reliable stand-alone reject determinant.

## 1. The authoritative taxonomy: AAPM TG-305 (2023)

**Primary source.** Little KJ, Reiser I, Apgar B, et al. (AAPM Task Group 305). "AAPM task group report 305: Guidance for standardization of vendor-neutral reject analysis in radiography." *Journal of Applied Clinical Medical Physics*, 2023, DOI 10.1002/acm2.13938 (PMID 36995917). A companion proposed IHE profile for implementers is at DOI 10.5281/zenodo.7783214. TG-305 explicitly extends AAPM Report 151 (acceptance testing / QC of digital radiography systems) by adding standard data elements and a vendor-neutral dataflow.

**Definitions (the exact consensus definitions).**

- **Reject / rejected acquisition / rejected image:** "Acquisitions of patient anatomy that are discarded by the technologist without being presented for diagnostic interpretation in any form to the radiologist or interpreting physician." An *acquisition* = one actuation of the x-ray exposure button, which can produce one or more raw (for-processing) images (dual energy, stitching, tomosynthesis). This definition is consistent with AAPM Report 151.
- **Repeat / repeated radiograph / re-take:** "Additional radiographs beyond the minimum anatomic views specified in the protocol. All repeats are sent for diagnostic review by a radiologist." Repeats can be legitimate (e.g., body habitus exceeds detector size) or radiologist-requested. **Repeats are explicitly outside the scope of TG-305.**
- **Reject rate (RR):** RR = Nrej / Ntot, where Nrej is the number of exposure button presses that do not produce a diagnostic image and Ntot is the total number of exposure button presses. For single-exposure acquisitions the number of presses equals the number of images; for multi-acquisition exams it does not. **Practitioner-directed repeats (category 8) and non-patient/QC exposures (category 9) are excluded from the calculation.** Reprocessed images are not acquisitions and are excluded from the denominator.

**The standardized reject-reason taxonomy.** TG-305 proposes a coarse-level schema (Table 3 / proposed DICOM CID 7011 updates, Table A.2), with fine-grained sub-categories that vendors may customize. The nine coarse categories:

1. **Patient positioning** — sub-reasons include rotation/tilt and cut-off / cone-cut (anatomy falling outside the sensitive detector area or x-ray field).
2. **Patient motion** — blur from anatomy moving during exposure or uncompensated source motion.
3. **Image artifacts** — signals that do not faithfully reproduce anatomy.
4. **Image contrast or noise** — low contrast, unacceptable noise/underexposure, saturation/overexposure; includes incorrect kVp, non-optimized processing, wrong grid or SID.
5. **Incorrect selection (protocol, detector, body part, patient)** — wrong anatomy/view/detector chosen on the system.
6. **Wrong body part or patient imaged** — mismatch with the ordered exam.
7. **Equipment failure** — equipment failed despite correct operation.
8. **Practitioner directed** — repeats at a physician's direction (excluded from RR).
9. **No patient exposure / test** — QC or research exposures (excluded from RR).

**Data elements.** TG-305 specifies minimum recorded fields with priority scores: priority 1 (essential for basic rates: reject status, reject reason, exposure count per acquisition, device/technologist identifiers), priority 2 (per-exam-type breakdown; e.g., body part, view, ordered exam), and priority 3 (needed for protocol optimization and exposure index analysis). Vendors should provide all fields regardless of priority and should auto-export a single combined acquisition+reject log to a server at regular intervals. Rejected images should remain retrievable for review.

**Reportable metrics.** TG-305's reportable metric is the reject rate per exposure press, plus reject-reason tallies and breakdowns by exam/technologist/device. It deliberately does **not** mandate a single target number; the report documents the spread of society-proposed targets (e.g., the Conference of Radiation Control Program Directors' Next Steps guidance suggests ~8% target with 10% upper and 5% lower thresholds, as cited in the report and in Serra et al. 2024) and leaves action thresholds to the facility and its qualified medical physicist. It also carries the benchmark history that frames interpretation: screen-film reject rates ~10-15% (with 40-45% of rejects due to over/underexposure), a reported drop to 2.3% when one site moved film→CR (Peer et al., as cited in TG-305), and **DR flat-panel reject rates of 11-13% across several studies with positioning the leading cause** — evidence that digitalization did not eliminate rejects (with Waaler's hypothesis that low-cost "take one more image" behavior raised DR rates; Waaler & Hofmann, *Radiation Protection Dosimetry*, 2010, DOI 10.1093/rpd/ncq032).

## 2. Practical measurement methodology: running reject analysis (RCA) in DR

**Where the data comes from.** Reject analysis draws on four sources: (a) vendor acquisition-workstation reject logs (which capture rejected images and, in some systems, exposure data); (b) RIS order/exam records; (c) PACS storage events (for deletion detection and for "repeats" — extra images stored for the same order); and (d) QA dashboards that combine these. TG-305's recommended workflow is the vendor-neutral log export into a single analytics store; a concrete vendor-agnostic implementation is the Australian multi-centre study below, which pulled CSVs/XML from 44 systems across 11 hospitals via USB export.

**Denominator conventions — the single most important comparability issue.** TG-305 defines RR per exposure button press, excluding practitioner-directed and QC/test exposures. But nearly all published DR studies compute rates on an *image* basis from logs or PACS, and definitions vary:

- Serra et al. 2024 define overall reject rate = rejected images / total acquired images, and further distinguish **absolute reject rate** (rejected images of a body part ÷ *all* images acquired — its share of the whole workload) from **relative reject rate** (rejected images of a body part ÷ images acquired *of that same body part* — how often that view fails). Only categories with ≥20 images were analyzed.
- Foos et al. 2009 computed reject rate = rejected images ÷ (rejected + accepted images) after filtering non-patient records.
- Hofmann et al. 2015 measured a *deletion rate* on workstations/PACS and argue the true reject+retake rate is higher because many rejected images are never deleted.
- Prieto et al. 2009 computed a *retake rate* from PACS by matching same-patient/same-projection/same-date images — i.e., a repeat-rate proxy measured on *accepted* images only.

The practical consequence: a study denominator of "all exposures" (TG-305 ideal) yields a higher rate than "accepted images" (many studies), and "rejected ÷ (rejected+accepted)" sits in between. Cross-study comparisons are only meaningful when the denominator is stated.

**How positioning vs exposure vs artifact are separated in practice.** Classification is a human assignment by the technologist at the moment of rejection, from a vendor or department drop-down list (e.g., the ten TG-151/TG-305-aligned reasons used by Ali & Yaseen 2021; the vendor defaults used by Bantas 2023; the custom lists in Foos 2009). Exposure problems are increasingly diagnosed *independently* of the reason code using the exposure index (see Section 5): Foos et al. 2009 showed that ~30% of images coded "underexposure" had normal EI (they were actually rendering artifacts from poor positioning of the abdomen into the field) and another ~20% with normal EI had noise from stale/un-erased CR plates — i.e., reason codes mislead without EI cross-checking. Motion vs positioning is likewise ambiguous: Foos documented that "patient motion" labels covered both true blur and stationary-but-moved positioning. This is why TG-305 recommends periodically reviewing a sample of rejected images against their coded reasons.

**Known methodological pitfalls (documented in the literature):**

- **Undercounting.** Near-zero reported reject rates usually signal under-reporting, not excellence (TG-305; DRPS synthesis). Rejects are hidden when a technologist re-shoots without flagging the first image; Hofmann et al. 2015 document that stored-but-unlabeled images inflate real reject+retake counts.
- **Non-patient contamination.** Foos et al. 2009 had to filter erasures and phantom/test exposures, removing 25-38% of raw reject records before computing rates — failure to filter distorts both numerator and denominator.
- **Automated detection overcounts.** Prieto et al. 2009's DICOM-header retake heuristic flagged 6.6% abdomen / 1.9% chest images as repetitions; expert review cut these to 3.3% / 0.9%, because reprocessed images, wrong patient/projection identifications, and two-plate-large-patient acquisitions were counted as retakes. Heuristic rates are not ground truth.
- **Reason-code subjectivity and inter-observer variability.** Reason assignment is self-reported and unvalidated in most studies; different technologists assign different codes to identical images (Foos 2009's motion/underexposure analysis; Bantas 2023's department-to-department differences even on identical Philips software). Radiographers reject at higher rates than radiologists judge necessary (Saade et al., as cited in Liu et al. 2024/2025), and the two groups weight technical vs clinical criteria differently.
- **Coding confounds (AP vs PA, portable vs in-department).** Foos et al. 2009 found in-department chest reject rate of 9% vs 1% for portable chest — a ninefold difference driven by point-of-capture image review — and flagged that many "chest AP" rejects were actually portable exams under a default body-part code. Chest numbers that mix AP/PA and portable/in-department are not comparable.
- **Infrastructure gaps.** EI and reject info may live in separate vendor files without a common key (TG-305); EIT/DI may not be configured at all (HMC group, 2022, found DI calculable in only 38% of systems surveyed).

**Benchmark numbers from representative DR studies (all per-image deletion/reject rates unless noted).**

- **Serra D, Neep MJ, Ryan E.** "Multi-centre digital radiography reject analysis for different clinical room use types: The establishment of local reject reference levels for public hospital departments." *Journal of Medical Radiation Sciences*, 2024, 71(3):412-420, DOI 10.1002/jmrs.796. 44 systems, 11 Australian hospitals, 2,031,713 acquired + 172,495 rejected images. Median reject rate 9.1%; local reject reference level (LRRL, 75th percentile) 10.6%; by room type: emergency 7.4%, inpatients+outpatients 9.6%, outpatients 9.2%, hybrid 10.1%; highest absolute rates chest 2.1% and knee 1.4%; highest relative rates knee 18.1% and pelvis 17.2%; reasons: patient positioning 76%, patient motion 7.5%. Recommended benchmarking method: LRRL = 75th percentile of room rates (DRL-style), rederived periodically.
- **Bantas G, Sweeney RJ, Mdletshe S.** "Digital radiography reject analysis: A comparison between two radiology departments in New Zealand." *Journal of Medical Radiation Sciences*, 2023, 70(2):137-144, DOI 10.1002/jmrs.654. 76,325 images (Philips DDR, mandatory reason entry); RR 7.86% and 5.91%; positioning errors 79.4% / 77.3% of rejects.
- **Oudiz A (et al.).** "Digital radiography reject analysis of examinations with multiple rejects: an Australian emergency imaging department clinical audit." *Journal of Medical Radiation Sciences*, 2021, DOI 10.1002/jmrs.468. 11,596 images; overall RR 10.3%; multiple-reject rate 1.3%; top reasons positioning 58.0% and anatomy cut-off 18.3%; multiple-reject reasons positioning 67.1%, cut-off 8.4%.
- **Hofmann B, Rosanowsky TB, Jensen CMA, Wah KHC.** "Image rejects in general direct digital radiography." *Acta Radiologica Open*, 2015, 4(10):2058460115604339, DOI 10.1177/2058460115604339. Deletion rate 11.0% (596/5417 images, January 2014, Norway); positioning 51.3% + centering 31.0% (82.3% combined); collimation 6.4%, artifacts 2.2%, wrong exposure 0.5%; highest per-exam knee 20.6%, hip 18.5%, ankle 13.8%.
- **Foos DH, Sehnert WJ, Reiner B, Siegel EL, Segal A, Waldman DL.** "Digital Radiography Reject Analysis: Data Collection Methodology, Results, and Recommendations from an In-depth Investigation at Two Hospitals." *Journal of Digital Imaging*, 2009, 22(1):89-98, DOI 10.1007/s10278-007-9089-5 (PMC3043667). ~288,000 CR records, forced reason entry; RR 4.4% (university) and 4.9% (community); positioning + anatomy cut-off 45-56% of rejects; improper exposure 13-14%; motion 7-11%; in-department chest 9% vs portable 1%. Definitive source on methodology, filtering, and EI-based reason validation.
- **Prieto C, Vañó E, Ten JI, Fernández JM, Íñiguez AI, Arévalo N, Litcheva A, Crespo E, Floriano A, Martínez D.** "Image Retake Analysis in Digital Radiography Using DICOM Header Information." *Journal of Digital Imaging*, 2009, 22(4):393-399, DOI 10.1007/s10278-008-9135-y (PMC3043704). PACS-based automatic retake detection: heuristic 6.6% abdomen / 1.9% chest → verified 3.3% / 0.9% (see pitfalls above).
- **Waaler D, Hofmann B.** "Image rejects/retakes: radiographic challenges." *Radiation Protection Dosimetry*, 2010, 139(1-3):375-379, DOI 10.1093/rpd/ncq032. Conceptual frame: reject rate understates dose waste because rejects without retakes, and un-deleted rejects, are invisible to most systems.

## 3. Published reject/repeat numbers for chest PA and pelvis AP

### Chest PA

The field rarely reports chest PA in isolation — many studies report "chest" (mixing PA, AP, and portable) — and the distinction matters because AP/portable chest rejects at several times the PA rate. Separating the numbers that exist:

- **Oudiz et al. 2021 (JMRS, ED DR audit):** per-projection deletion rates PA chest 5.0% (129/2564), AP chest 18.1% (175/966), lateral chest 5.8% (131/2250). PA chest is among the *lowest*-reject projections; AP chest among the highest.
- **Liaqat R, Majeed AI, Malik MN, et al.** "Confronting our mistakes: A comprehensive evaluation of radiographic errors in digital chest radiography among adult population in a public sector hospital." *Annals of PIMS*, 2021, 17(2), DOI 10.48036/apims.v17i2.440. 1,560 digital chest X-rays scored by two residents; overall rejection rate 16.5%; error frequencies: positioning error 44.5%, poor collimation 29.8%, artifacts 14.9%, improper exposure 8%, motion blur 2.2%, mislabeling 0.5%; leading rejection cause was anatomy cut-off, especially the cardiophrenic (CP) angle. (Resident-judged quality on *accepted* PACS images, so it measures error prevalence, not true deletion rate.)
- **King Fahad Hospital audit.** "Evaluation of Reject Analysis of Chest Radiographs in Diagnostic Radiology." *American Journal of Diagnostic Imaging*, 2019 (retrospective, n=150 CXRs; smaller and less rigorous source). Rejection rate 14.7%; positioning 16%, artifacts 11.3%; scapular shadow partially in lung field in 38%.
- **Prieto et al. 2009 (J Digit Imaging):** verified PACS repetition rate for chest 0.9% (accepted images only — a *repeat* proxy, not a deletion rate).
- **Jabbari N, Zeinali A, Rahmatnezhad L.** "Patient dose from radiographic rejects/repeats in radiology centers of Urmia University of Medical Sciences, Iran." *Health*, 2012, DOI 10.4236/health.2012.42015. Screen-film-era repeat rates: chest 10.85%, overall 7.20%; causes exposure 54%, positioning 18% — the pre-digital contrast for today's positioning-dominated distribution.
- **Songklanakarin Hospital (Thai Journal of Radiological Technology, ~2023):** chest PA upright retake rate 1.96% (before intervention); chest reject reasons: positioning/breathing 66.7%, other 14.2%, artifacts 9.9%, motion 8.5%. Direct evidence that **inadequate inspiration ("breathing") is a first-class chest rejection cause**, consistent with the dedicated inspiration studies in Section 4.

**Top causes for chest PA, across studies:** positioning (esp. rotation and scapular overlap of lung fields), inadequate inspiration / breath-hold failure, poor collimation (CP-angle cut-off is the canonical failure), artifacts, and (rarely, in DR) exposure. Positioning accounts for roughly half of chest reject causes; inspiration is a well-documented sub-cause.

### Pelvis AP

Pelvis is repeatedly identified as one of the highest-reject projections in DR, with reasons concentrated on rotation/tilt and anatomy cut-off.

- **Lee S, Gray F, Jimenez Y, Said S, Moore C.** "Positional Challenges of the Anteroposterior Pelvic X-Ray: Comparison of Imaging Reject Rates Between Trauma Trolley and Table Bucky." *Journal of Medical Radiation Sciences*, 2025, 72(3):376-384, DOI 10.1002/jmrs.70004 (PMC12420655). 1,847 ED pelvis X-rays over 12 months, Australia. Mean reject rate 35.5% on trauma trolley vs 18.8% on table bucky (p<0.001); **first-exposure accuracy 56.7% vs 81.8%**; superior and lateral cut-off were the dominant missing margins (70% of cut-off anatomy on trolley, 82% on bucky); smaller trauma-trolley detectors (35x43 cm vs 43x43 cm) and non-standard SID implicated. Reviews prior pelvis reject rates as ranging 5-23%, with the overall DR range ~0.02-15%.
- **Oudiz et al. 2021 (JMRS):** AP pelvis deletion rate 22.5% (78/347) in the ED audit, with pelvis/hip overall 23.8%.
- **Serra et al. 2024 (JMRS):** pelvis has the second-highest *relative* reject rate, 17.2% (knee 18.1% highest) — i.e., ~1 in 6 pelvis acquisitions is deleted.
- **Foos et al. 2009 (J Digit Imaging):** pelvis among the exam types with CR reject rates ≥8% (with skull/facial bones, shoulder, hip, spine, in-department chest).
- **Hofmann et al. 2015 (Acta Radiol Open):** pelvis+hip deletion rate 8.2%; pelvis rejects were 62.2% centering errors — the pelvis is dominated by centering/cut-off rather than the rotation-dominant hip.
- **Jabbari et al. 2012 (Health, film-era):** pelvis had the highest repeat rate, 14.01%.
- **Tzeng et al.** (cited in Oudiz 2021) and **Hofmann & Rosanowsky et al.** report pelvis/hip and knee/hip as the highest-reject exams (knee 20.6%, hip 18.5% in Hofmann 2015).
- **Positioning-error mechanics (why pelvis fails):** centring-point and axial-rotation variability. **Mellor FE, Smith L, England A, Snaith B, Cosson P.** "A retrospective evaluation of supine pelvic radiography image quality using centring points and anatomical axial rotation, including reliability of measurements (ARLEX-P STUDY)." *Radiography*, 2023, 29(5):941-949, DOI 10.1016/j.radi.2023.07.002. From 380 images: median centring-point deviation +22 mm (range about +2 to +43 mm) relative to the inter-acetabular line, 66% of images centred inferiorly, median pelvic axial rotation 0.0° (IQR about -1.5° to +1.4°) with greater rotation variance in males; inter-observer ICC for the key measurements ranged 0.24-0.87, i.e., even the *measurement* of pelvis positioning is only moderately reliable — relevant to any automated system's ground-truth labelling. **STOPPAGE study** (Mellor FE, England A, Cosson P, et al., "Comparison of image quality, radiation dose and repeat rates for AP pelvic radiography imaging techniques," *Radiography*, 2025, DOI 10.1016/j.radi.2025.103275) compares AP pelvis acquisition techniques on exactly these outcomes (image quality, dose, repeat rates).

**Top causes for pelvis AP:** positioning (rotation/tilt), anatomy cut-off (superior and lateral margins), centring-point error, artifacts, suboptimal exposure (rare in DR), and — uniquely for trauma — the trolley/table setup itself (Lee 2025). Rotation and tilt are the diagnostic-critical failures because they distort acetabular landmarks used for surgical planning (see also the acetabular-orientation positioning-device trial: Brockmeyer et al., *J Exp Orthop*, 2019, DOI 10.1186/s40634-019-0191-7, which found mechanical positioning devices did not improve pelvis AP accuracy — evidence that the pelvis problem is not solved by hardware aids).

## 4. AI and automation in reject analysis

Two families of work exist: (a) **automated classification of acquisition quality / reject-worthiness from the finished radiograph** (post-exposure), and (b) automated **audit tools** (DICOM-header retake detection, EI monitoring). No published work predicts reject-worthiness **pre-exposure** from a live optical/positioning feed, which is the copilot's open niche.

**Post-exposure quality classification (primary sources):**

- **Dunnmon JA, Yi D, Langlotz CP, Ré C, Rubin DL, Lungren MP.** "Assessment of Convolutional Neural Networks for Automated Classification of Chest Radiographs." *Radiology*, 2018, 290(2):537-544, DOI 10.1148/radiol.2018181422. Foundational demonstration that CNNs can classify CXR technical adequacy/quality automatically, motivating the whole subfield.
- **Liu E, Carrion D, Badawy MK.** "Using Convolutional Neural Networks for the Classification of Suboptimal Chest Radiographs." medRxiv preprint 2024, DOI 10.1101/2024.11.10.24317051; peer-reviewed version in *Journal of Medical Radiation Sciences*, 2025, DOI 10.1002/jmrs.70006. DenseNet121 AUROC 0.97, YOLOv8 0.95 for optimal-vs-suboptimal CXR (PA, AP, and lateral included); authors explicitly note radiographers reject more aggressively than radiologists, and that reason *sub-classification* (anatomical exclusion, exposure, poor inspiration, mispositioning, overlying anatomy) is a future need.
- **SQUIRE** (shoulder). "Automated Shoulder Radiograph Quality Review to Support Efficient Workflow in the Emergency Department: The SQUIRE Deep Learning Ensemble." *Journal of Imaging Informatics in Medicine*, 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10278-026-01903-5. Real-time, lightweight (<50M-param) ensemble classifying Grashey/axillary shoulder views as diagnostic vs repeat-needed; internal F1 0.943 / accuracy 0.941; external zero-shot ROC-AUC 0.82, few-shot 0.85; **releases the first public expert-verified shoulder acquisition-quality labels on MURA** (732 images) and code. Closest published analogue to a point-of-care quality copilot — but it is image-post-acquisition, shoulder-only, and its own data show marked domain-shift degradation across institutions.
- **Sekiura K, Yoshimura T, Sugimori H.** "Development of an XAI-Enhanced Deep-Learning Algorithm for Automated Decision-Making on Shoulder-Joint X-Ray Retaking." *Applied Sciences*, 2025, 15(19):10534, DOI 10.3390/app151910534. Two-stage YOLOX localization + classifier for shoulder true-AP quality: detector mAP@0.5 = 1.00, classifier AUC 0.977 (F1 0.943); Grad-CAM attention aligned with expert regions.
- **Sorace L, Raju N, O'Shaughnessy J, et al.** "Assessment of inspiration and technical quality in anteroposterior thoracic radiographs using machine learning." *Radiography*, 2024, 30(1), DOI 10.1016/j.radi.2023.10.014. ML assessment of **inspiration adequacy** and technical quality in AP thoracic radiographs — direct relevance to the chest-inspiration reject cause.
- **Nousiainen K, Mäkelä T, Piilonen A, Peltonen J.** "Automating chest radiograph imaging quality control." *Physica Medica*, 2021, 83:138-145, DOI 10.1016/j.ejmp.2021.03.014. Automated CXR QC (collimation, inspiration, etc.).
- **Yu M, et al.** "Automated quality assessment of chest radiographs based on deep learning and linear regression cascade algorithms." *European Radiology*, 2022, 32(11):7680-7690, DOI 10.1007/s00330-022-08771-x. Multi-level automated CXR quality assessment.
- Also: **Costa M, et al.**, "Deep Feature-Based Automated Chest Radiography Compliance Assessment," *IEEE ENBENG*, 2023, DOI 10.1109/ENBENG58165.2023.10175341 (retrieves up to 81% of non-compliant images via deep features); and a Chinese thesis-scale chest DR quality system (5 primary / 20 secondary quality indices, layout/position/gray-scale/sharpness/foreign-body) summarized at globethesis.com (2022-2023) — evidence that commercial-style chest QC systems decompose quality into position/collimation/grayscale/sharpness dimensions, the same sub-attributes a copilot would score.

**Automated audit tooling:** Prieto et al. 2009 (DICOM-header retake detection, Section 2) and the EI-tracking programs of Section 5 are the established automation for *auditing*, not for real-time intervention. No RCT or prospective study yet demonstrates that an automated point-of-care quality system lowers reject rates in routine DR; the SQUIRE paper explicitly calls multi-institutional prospective validation "future work." The intervention literature that *does* show reject reduction is training/feedback audits (e.g., the Thai chest/knee guideline study reducing knee retakes 17.11%→16.10%, *Thai Journal of Radiological Technology*, ~2023).

## 5. Exposure index (EI) and deviation index (DI)

**Standards.** IEC 62494-1:2008 ("Medical electrical equipment - Exposure index of digital X-ray imaging systems - Part 1: Definitions and requirements for general radiography," Geneva: IEC) defines EI (proportional to incident detector air kerma under a reference RQA-5 beam), target EI (EIT), and deviation index DI = 10·log10(EI/EIT). AAPM Report 116 (2009, "An Exposure Indicator for Digital Radiography") provides the same concept with DI = 10·log10(KIND/KTGT), and adds a published DI control-limit table: -0.5 to +0.5 = target; beyond ±1.0 = investigate; **DI < -1.0 = "underexposed: consult radiologist for repeat."** DI ≈ +1 = ~26% overexposure; -1 ≈ 20% underexposure; ±3 ≈ doubling/halving of detector exposure (Seibert & Morin, "New Exposure Indicators for Digital Radiography," *AJR*, 2013, DOI 10.2214/AJR.12.8678).

**How EI/DI relate to technique quality and rejects.** EI quantifies what the *detector* received, not what the patient absorbed, and it is computed from a segmented region of interest: anything that corrupts segmentation (off-centering, poor collimation, wrong anatomy menu, prostheses, multiple body parts) produces a misleading EI/DI (Seibert & Morin 2013). That makes EI/DI a good *cross-check* on reject reasons but a poor stand-alone reject predictor. The documented relationships:

- **Reason validation:** Foos et al. 2009 used EI distributions to show coded "underexposure" rejects often had normal EI (rendering artifact from poor positioning; stale-plate noise) — EI audits catch misclassified rejects.
- **QC / exposure-creep monitoring:** **Scott A, Zhou Y, Allahverdian J, Nute J, Lee CS.** "Evaluation of digital radiography practice using exposure index tracking." *J Appl Clin Med Phys*, 2016, 17(6), DOI 10.1120/jacmp.v17i6.6082. EI histograms from four detectors revealed up to 400-unit average EI differences between units (≈60% radiation difference) and multi-peak distributions from calibration drift and lingering CR techniques; used EI medians/SD per protocol as a QC dashboard alongside reject rates.
- **Practice norms:** **AAPM TG-232 Report.** "Current State of Practice Regarding Digital Radiography Exposure Indicators and Deviation Indices." *Medical Physics*, 2018, DOI 10.1002/mp.13212. Across practice settings, typical DI distributions have SD 1.3-3.6, mean DI substantially ≠ 0, and **<50% of clinical DIs fell within the TG-116 ±1 limits** — TG-116's limits were too strict for real practice. TG-232 recommends targeting mean DI = 0 and using site-specific ±1 SD / ±2 SD action limits, reviewed continuously.
- **Feasibility gap in the field:** the HMC group ("From the use of exposure index in quality control testing to the use of exposure index for quality control of clinical images," *J Appl Clin Med Phys*, 2022, PMC9647429) surveyed clinical EIT/DI usage and found DI calculable in only 38% of systems (EIT never configured in the rest), plus EI-calibration errors (e.g., a vendor display overestimated EI by ~50%) and the fact that EI is blind to field-size and kVp problems that a KAP meter catches. Practical conclusion: EI/DI programs must be locally calibrated before they can be trusted.
- **EI as an outcome metric in reject studies:** EI/DI appear as *supporting* outcome metrics (EI validation of rejects; EI monitoring as QC), not as the primary endpoint in reject-rate trials — no study uses DI as its headline metric for a reject-reduction intervention. TG-305 itself lists EI analysis as a priority-3 (detailed-analysis) data element. For a copilot evaluation, EI/DI are therefore best used as a *confounding check* (to prove exposure practice did not change while positioning improved), not as the primary outcome.

---

## Gaps: what no one has measured

1. **Pre-exposure reject prediction.** No published work predicts reject-worthiness from a live camera/positioning feed before the x-ray is taken. Every automated system classifies the *finished* image (chest: Dunnmon 2018, Liu 2024/25, Nousiainen 2021, Sorace 2024, Yu 2022; shoulder: SQUIRE 2026, Sekiura 2025). The copilot's core value proposition — intervene before the exposure — is unmeasured territory.
2. **No public ground-truth quality labels for chest PA or pelvis AP acquisition quality.** SQUIRE released the first public shoulder acquisition-quality labels (MURA subset, 2026); the equivalent does not exist for the two projections the copilot targets, and the only reliability data (ARLEX-P 2023) show inter-observer ICC as low as 0.24 for pelvis centring metrics — ground truth for evaluation must be built and its observer reliability quantified.
3. **Radiographer vs radiologist disagreement is quantified only obliquely.** The field knows radiographers reject more aggressively than radiologists (Saade et al., cited in Liu 2024/25; Bantas 2023's department differences), but no study measures this on chest PA / pelvis AP with a copilot in the loop, i.e., whether the tool should optimize toward the technologist's or the radiologist's quality bar.
4. **Denominator comparability.** Almost all published DR rates are per-image deletion rates from logs or PACS, not TG-305's per-exposure-press reject rate, and none separate "reject" from "repeat" quantitatively in the same dataset (TG-305 excludes repeats; Prieto 2009 approximates repeats; the two metrics are never jointly reported from one data source).
5. **No prospective, controlled trial of a point-of-care AI quality system reducing reject rates** in routine DR; the closest published causal evidence is training/feedback audits (Thai chest/knee guideline study; Foos 2009's intervention recommendations) with small or pre/post designs.
6. **Live, per-cause attribution.** No study ties live optical metrics (patient rotation angle, tilt, inspiration depth, predicted collimation field) to actual rejection outcomes and dose saved — the link between pre-exposure measurable geometry and post-exposure rejection is assumed, not measured.
7. **Portable/AP chest contamination.** Reported "chest" rates routinely mix PA, AP, and portable acquisitions (Foos 2009), so clean PA-chest benchmarks with a stated denominator do not exist at scale.
